Learn: Beginner Examples

Simple, picture-first examples: a trading day, reading a candlestick chart, a sample trade, and position sizing.

Everything on this page is a hypothetical EXAMPLE. EXMPL is a made-up ticker and every chart uses fictional data. Nothing here is a recommendation or financial advice.

1. What an intraday trade is

An intraday trade starts and ends on the same day. You begin the day holding nothing, buy at some point after the open, sell later, and are back to zero shares (flat) before the market closes.

If you are still holding shares at the close, it is no longer an intraday trade. You are exposed to overnight news and price gaps.

2. How to read a basic chart

Most trading charts use candlesticks. Each candle shows what the price did during one time period, for example 5 minutes.

  • Open: the price at the start of the period.
  • Close: the price at the end of the period.
  • High / Low: the highest and lowest prices reached during the period.
  • Body: the thick part, between the open and the close.
  • Wicks (shadows): the thin lines above and below the body, reaching the high and the low.
  • Green candle: the price closed higher than it opened. Red candle: the price closed lower than it opened.
  • Volume bars (bottom): how many shares traded in each period. Tall bars mean lots of activity.

3. Sample trade walkthrough (EXAMPLE)

Here is a hypothetical trade on the fictional ticker EXMPL. The plan is written down before buying.

Outcome A: target reached

  1. Buy 50 shares of EXMPL at $20.00. Cost: 50 × $20.00 = $1,000.
  2. Immediately place a stop-loss at $19.60 and a sell order (target) at $20.80.
  3. Price rises during the day and reaches $20.80, so the target order sells all 50 shares.
  4. Result: 50 × ($20.80 − $20.00) = +$40.00 (before fees). The position is closed, so you are flat before the close.

Outcome B: stop-loss hit

  1. Same plan: buy 50 shares at $20.00, stop at $19.60, target at $20.80.
  2. This time the price falls after you buy.
  3. The price touches $19.60 and the stop-loss sells all 50 shares.
  4. Result: 50 × ($19.60 − $20.00) = −$20.00 (before fees). The loss is exactly what you planned, not more.

With a 1:2 risk/reward, one win (+$40) covers two losses (−$20 each). But in fast markets a stop can fill worse than planned (called slippage), and fees reduce every result.

4. Position sizing: risk 1% of a $5,000 account

Position sizing answers one question: how many shares should I buy so a losing trade only costs a small, planned amount?

  1. Hypothetical account size: $5,000.
  2. Risk 1% per trade: $5,000 × 1% = $50 maximum loss.
  3. Risk per share = entry − stop = $20.00 − $19.60 = $0.40.
  4. Shares = $50 ÷ $0.40 = 125 shares.
  5. Position value = 125 × $20.00 = $2,500, but only $50 is at risk if the stop works as planned.

Even with good sizing, losses add up. Practise with a simulator or paper-trading account before using real money.
